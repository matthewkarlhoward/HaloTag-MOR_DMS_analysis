## Structural analysis

Structural analyses used 41 experimental μOR structures from the PDB, the
μOR–buprenorphine–miniG<sub>oi</sub>12 structure determined here, and four Chai-1
predictions. For every structure a single receptor chain was selected and manually
verified, excluding duplicate copies in the asymmetric unit and all G protein, arrestin
and nanobody chains.

**Numbering.** All residues are reported in human OPRM1 numbering. Mouse structures were
offset by **+2** to align with human (mouse Asp147 = human Asp149), and the harmonised
`residue_number_human` was used as the join key between structural and mutational data
throughout. Superscripts follow the Ballesteros–Weinstein generic numbering scheme as
assigned by GPCRdb.

**Ligand contact shells.** For each μOR–ligand complex, receptor residues were assigned to
contact shells by side-chain proximity. A residue was assigned to the **first shell** if
any of its side-chain atoms lay within **5.0 Å** of any ligand atom, and to the **second
shell** if it was not first shell and any of its side-chain atoms lay within **4.5 Å** of
a first-shell residue's side-chain atoms. Glycine and alanine backbone atoms were treated
as side chain where no side chain is present. **[CONFIRM — check how the code handles Gly]**

**Pose prediction.** Poses for butorphanol, methadone, nalbuphine and naltrexone were
predicted with Chai-1 using MSAs generated by MMseqs2. Each prediction comprised
full-length wild-type human μOR (chain A), Gα<sub>i</sub> (chain B) and the ligand
(chain C) supplied as SMILES; naloxone was additionally predicted without Gα<sub>i</sub>.
The top-ranked model was used for contact assignment, except for naltrexone where rank 1
was used. **[CONFIRM — why rank 1 for naltrexone; and add the Chai-1 version]**

**G protein interface residues.** Receptor–Gα contacts were assigned with the same
side-chain criterion used for ligands: first shell ≤ 5.0 Å from any Gα atom, second shell
≤ 4.5 Å from a first-shell side chain. Active-state contacts were pooled across the 29
G protein-bound active structures; latent/intermediate-state contacts were taken from
9ODL and 9PXW, in which the Gα<sub>i</sub> C terminus engages extrahelically. Each
intracellular position was then classified as contacting G protein in the active state
only, the intermediate state only, or both (Figure 7c). Structure state assignments
(34 active, 6 inactive, 1 intermediate) are given in the deposited
`metadata/pdb_state_table.csv`.

**Coordinates for network construction.** Cα coordinates for the residue network in
Figure 8 were taken from chain R of the active-state structure 8EFQ.

## Determination of G protein interface residues

Receptor–G protein contacts were determined with the same geometric criterion used for
ligand contacts. For every μOR structure containing a resolved Gα subunit, the minimum
all-atom and side-chain distance was computed from each receptor residue to Gα.
**First-shell** residues were those with a side-chain atom within **5.0 Å** of any Gα
atom; **second-shell** residues were those not already in the first shell with a
side-chain atom within **4.5 Å** of a first-shell residue's side chain. As elsewhere,
a single manually verified receptor chain and Gα chain were used per structure, and
structures without a resolved Gα were excluded.

Two contact sets were defined. The **active-state** set pooled first-shell positions
across the **29** G protein-bound active structures. The **latent/intermediate** set was
taken from **9ODL** and **9PXW**, in which the Gα<sub>i</sub> C terminus engages the
receptor extrahelically rather than inserting into the core; only Gα contacts were
counted for this set (Gβ contacts were excluded). Each intracellular position was then
classified as contacting G protein in the active state only, the intermediate state only,
or both.

## Intracellular sensitivity map

Candidate positions for Figure 7c were the union of the two contact sets above together
with every remaining ICL1, ICL2, ICL3 and helix 8 position, assigned from the GPCRdb
secondary-structure annotation. Positions in that last group contact G protein in
neither state and are labelled accordingly.

Candidates were retained for display if their mean missense effect across the fifteen
ligands fell more than **0.05** below the synonymous mean, giving the **30** positions
shown. The heatmap value is the **percentage of missense variants at that position that
are loss-of-function for that ligand**, where loss-of-function is an effect more than
**two standard deviations** below that ligand's own synonymous mean — the threshold is
computed per ligand, not pooled. Values run from 0% (no loss-of-function variants at that
position for that ligand) to 100% (all variants loss-of-function), on a sequential
white-to-red scale. Ligands are ordered by efficacy class and the ligand-free
(forskolin-only) condition is shown first.

No surface-expression filter was applied here, in contrast to the residue-network
analysis, where nodes are restricted to variants expressing near wild-type levels.

## Double-mutant TRUPATH BRET analysis

**Constructs.** Double mutants combining A119L<sup>2.53</sup> with K100D<sup>12.49</sup>,
K100N<sup>12.49</sup>, V175N<sup>34.51</sup>, R278D<sup>6.31</sup>, I280A<sup>6.33</sup>
or I280K<sup>6.33</sup> were generated in pTwist-CMV-μOR by Q5 site-directed mutagenesis
(NEB) and verified by sequencing. **[CONFIRM — Q5 assumed by analogy with the single
mutants; add the sequencing method]**

**Measurements.** TRUPATH Gi1 BRET was performed as described above against DAMGO, PZM21,
nalbuphine and naloxone. Data were acquired in two independent experiments: one covering
K100D, K100N and R278D (4 biological replicates, 10 ligand concentrations) and one
covering V175N, I280A and I280K (6 biological replicates, 9 concentrations). Wild-type
μOR and A119L were included in both and pooled across them (n = 10). The two experiments
were co-scaled on their shared wild-type and A119L arms (r = 0.98) and were **not**
renormalised to DAMGO, since wild type is the noisiest anchor. All variants used here
expressed within two-fold of wild-type μOR by cell-surface flow cytometry (supplemental
figure 11b).

**Curve fitting.** The assay reads signal-down: baseline BRET ratio ≈ 1 and activation
decreases the ratio, so the activation window of a curve is 1 − Top = −Span. Replicate
means at each concentration were fit with an unconstrained three-parameter sigmoid,
`response = Bottom + (Top − Bottom) / (1 + 10^(logEC50 − log[A]))`, with Bottom bounded to
[0.8, 1.2], Top to [0.0, 1.3] and logEC50 to [−13, −4]. The fit was not constrained in
direction, so a genuine small upward response was permitted.

**Testing for a dose-response.** Because a flat line is the sigmoid with Top = Bottom, the
two models are nested, and we tested whether the sigmoid was justified by an
extra-sum-of-squares F test (F = [(SS_flat − SS_sigmoid)/2] / [SS_sigmoid/(n − 3)],
compared against F<sub>2, n−3</sub>). Curves with p < 0.05 were considered responsive and
their fitted Span retained regardless of sign; curves with p ≥ 0.05 were reported as no
response, with Span set to 0 and drawn as a flat line at the data mean. This replaces a
fixed R² threshold, which rejects real shallow curves, and a sign constraint, which
forces a response where none exists.

**Quantification.** Efficacy is reported as percent activation relative to the wild-type
DAMGO window: 100 × (−Span) / (−Span of wild type with DAMGO). Uncertainties are the SEM
of the activation window across replicate-level fits. A small negative percentage
therefore denotes a significant but upward, non-activating curve rather than negative
efficacy.
